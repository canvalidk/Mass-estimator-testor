## 1. The linear maps allowed by the symmetry

Use the Euclidean metric $\delta$ of physical space. For a group of orthogonal maps $G$, write
$\operatorname{End}_G(\mathbb R^k)=\{M:MR=RM\text{ for every }R\in G\}$. Then

| $k$ | $\operatorname{End}_{SO(k)}$ | $\operatorname{End}_{O(k)}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $\mathbb R I$ | $\mathbb R I$ |
| $2$ | $\{pI+qJ:p,q\in\mathbb R\}\cong\mathbb C$ | $\mathbb R I$ |
| $3$ | $\mathbb R I$ | $\mathbb R I$ |

This is the commutant in real Schur's lemma. The commutant of an irreducible
real representation is a division algebra and can be real, complex or quaternionic.
[Ziller, *Lie Groups. Representation Theory and Symmetric Spaces*, Lemma 5.1, p. 93](https://www2.math.upenn.edu/~wziller/math650/LieGroupsReps.pdf).

For $k=1$ all linear maps are scalars. In the oriented plane let $J$ be rotation
by $\pi/2$. Commuting with $J$ forces $pI+qJ$, which commutes with every planar
rotation and corresponds to multiplication by $p+iq$. A reflection $S$ sends
$SJS^{-1}=-J$, so invariance under even one reflection forces $q=0$.
Simultaneous reversal of both axes is $-I$, a planar rotation that commutes with
every map. Parity here means orientation reversal. If one separately assumes
self-adjoint inertia, $J^T=-J$ already gives $q=0$.
[Etingof, *Lie Groups and Lie Algebras*, §11, remark after Lemma 11.9, p. 64](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-755-lie-groups-and-lie-algebras-ii-spring-2024/mit18_755_s24_lec_full.pdf).

The standard $SO(3)$ representation is irreducible of **real type**. Rotations
act transitively on the sphere, giving real irreducibility. Any real $3\times3$
commuting map has a real eigenvalue, whose eigenspace is invariant and therefore
all of $\mathbb R^3$. Its complexification is the irreducible $V^{(1)}$.
[Mason, *Representation Theory Epiphany*, Corollary 3.15.6 and Example 3.15.7](https://www.maths.dur.ac.uk/users/jamie.j.mason/RepresentationTheoryNotes-epiphany/Ch3.S15.html).
The $O(3)$ commutant is contained in the $SO(3)$ commutant and contains all scalars.

These are symmetries of a fixed operator. Under passive changes of frame, a
general tensor still obeys $M\mapsto RMR^{-1}$ and gives the same physical
prediction in new coordinates. L16's bookkeeping alone supplies covariance.
The commutation claim uses L22's physical isotropy, with reflection invariance
needed in 2D when one starts with rotations and allows every linear map.

## 2. One planar pair

For the misfits use T70's independent isotropic channel noise, with
$f=F/\sigma_F$, $x=a/\sigma_a$. The reading metric is squared Euclidean distance
on $(f,x)$ in these noise units. A physical inertia map becomes $M$ after
multiplication by $\sigma_a/\sigma_F$, which preserves each operator class.
Take the infimum over the squared distance from the datum to the union of
graphs $(My,y)$ in each class. This is the whole-line comparison, before L1's
positive-mass restriction. The same exact-fit statements are metric-independent.

Identify the oriented plane with $\mathbb C$. If $x\ne0$, multiplication by
$f/x$ fits $(f,x)$ exactly, so the $SO(2)$-commutant gives zero misfit. If $x=0$
and $f\ne0$, using $M=tI$ and $y=f/t$ gives the same zero infimum as $t\to\infty$.
With $O(2)$, the graphs have $f_*=p x_*$. Their closure consists of column
pairs of rank at most one, including the vertical projective endpoint. The
least squared residual is

$$Q=\lambda_{\min}\!\begin{pmatrix}\|f\|^2& f\cdot x\\f\cdot x&\|x\|^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Indeed, projecting onto the candidate channel line of T36 (2) gives this
Rayleigh minimum in the perpendicular channel direction. It is the same $Q$
as `reading_state` and T70's distance to real states. In oriented complex
coordinates $(f,x)$ has that Gram as the real part of its outer product.
An anti-parallel pair has $Q=0$ and can have $Q_+>0$. Reality and positivity
are separate conditions.

## 3. One spatial push and its stabilizer

Let $e$ be a unit vector, $P_e=ee^T$, and $J_e y=e\times y$. The rotations
fixing $e$ act trivially on $\mathbb Re$ and by $SO(2)$ on $e^\perp$. Commuting
maps preserve the fixed line. Commutation with the half-turn about $e$ also
preserves its perpendicular plane, so the commutant is

$$\mathcal C_e=\{cP_e+p(I-P_e)+qJ_e:c,p,q\in\mathbb R\}\cong\mathbb R\oplus\mathbb C.$$

**Axis tied to the latent push.** If $y$ is the true acceleration and $e$
lies along it, $My=cy$. The transverse block is invisible. Varying the unknown
axis with the latent push and varying $c$ gives precisely the real scalar graph
family, so the misfit is $Q$ from §2 with 3D vectors. Near a nonzero true push,
collinearity imposes two transverse conditions (L8's $k-1$). A single aligned
push can only establish what the operator does along that direction. A known
push axis with $y\in\mathbb Re$ fixes this direction in the fit. Its unrestricted
real-scalar infimum is $\|(I-P_e)f\|^2+\|(I-P_e)x\|^2$, since the two axial
coordinates can be matched in closure.

**Axis independent of the latent push.** If $e$ may be chosen independently,
take it perpendicular to $\operatorname{span}(x,f)$ for $x\ne0$. The whole
pair is in the transverse plane, and choosing $p=(x\cdot f)/\|x\|^2$ and
$q=((e\times x)\cdot f)/\|x\|^2$ gives $Mx=f$ for any $c$. The misfit is zero
(as an infimum also when $x=0$). Even for an independently fixed $e$, generic
$x$ has both nonzero axial and planar components. Each component then fits
in its own block, and those exact fits are dense in all pairs.

B23's zero-misfit prediction applies to the independent-axis model. For the
literal push stabilizer take $x=e_3$, $f=e_1$. The tied-axis misfit is $Q=1$.
An independent axis $e=e_2$ gives an exact fit with $M=P_e+J_e$. The seeded
tests check both models on the same data.

## 4. Where the assumptions sit

L22 concerns physical isotropy in the Newton bin. L16 supplies frame bookkeeping
at the space/frame rung R0 of the Newton ladder. The assumption that inertia
itself is reflection-invariant belongs with the physical symmetry commitment
at R2. The orientation and reflection of the plane come from space. The
numerical $Q$ uses the measurement bin's noise metric. O17's nonscalar symmetric positive-definite
inertia breaks full operator isotropy and can have distinct principal gains.
The planar complex alternative preserves proper rotations, with $qJ$ a skew
response that breaks reflection symmetry when $q\ne0$. The alternatives have
different symmetry and positivity constraints.
