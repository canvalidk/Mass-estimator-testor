## Standard mathematics

A confidence distribution is a function of the parameter that is a CDF for each sample and uniformly
distributed at the true value (Schweder and Hjort 2002; Xie and Singh 2013). A signed pivot's rank is
the usual way to make one. It is one when the rank is monotone in the parameter, which is what fails
here, as in Fieller's problem of a ratio (Fieller 1954), of which the slope of a line is a relative.

## Statement

Scalar readings of one instrument in noise units, $G$ the series' matrix of dot products with
eigenvalues $L\ge l$. T45's signed score at the candidate angle $\theta$ is
$Z(\theta)=u_\theta^\top Gv_\theta/\sqrt{u_\theta^\top Gu_\theta}$, and its rank $C(\theta)=\Phi(-Z(\theta))$ is exactly uniform at the true
mass, given the readings' along-line parts.

**(1) The rank rises and falls.** $C$ rises where the candidate line holds more than the geometric mean
of the eigenvalues, $u_\theta^\top Gu_\theta>\sqrt{Ll}$ (an arc about the principal line), and falls elsewhere.

**(2) Over a full turn of the line of slopes it rises by**
$$W=2\,\Phi\big(\sqrt L-\sqrt l\big)-1$$
**and falls by the same.** $W<1$ on every finite series, $W=0$ when the eigenvalues tie (the all-zero
experiment; readings at right angles of equal size), and $W\to1$ as the readings identify a line.

**(3) So the exact tests are not the ordinates of one CDF.** On the positive masses the rising part is
$M\le W$. With $G=\operatorname{diag}(9,1)$ the rank is ½ at both ends of the positive masses and below ½
between them.

**What it measures.** Draw a rank uniformly and look for a mass where $C$ crosses it going up, among the
positive masses: one exists with probability exactly $M$. That is the share of a law of mass that the
exact tests supply with nothing added. The remaining $1-M$ has to come from somewhere else or be left
unsaid.

| series (noise units) | $W$ | $M$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the all-zero experiment | 0 | 0 |
| readings at right angles, equal sizes (scene C) | 0 | 0 |
| one reading at half a noise unit a channel | 0.52 | 0.38 |
| one reading at (3, 2) | 0.9997 | 0.976 |
| Can's near pair; his far pair | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |

## Proof

With $g(\theta)=\sqrt{u_\theta^\top Gu_\theta}$, $Z=g'$ and $g''=\det G/g^3-g$, so $C'=-\phi(Z)g''$ is positive
exactly where $g^4>\det G=Ll$. At the two ends of the rising arc $g^2=\sqrt{Ll}$ and
$Z=\pm(\sqrt L-\sqrt l)$ (T45 §3 has this as the largest value of the statistic), which gives (2).

## Against the rows

- **Is a point, or a law, owed under E13? [`../../problems/O20.md`, its first question]** A law read off
  the exact tests alone exists only to the share $M$. On the law-and-point line the starting law
  supplies the other $1-M$ (E12 builds exactly that mixture). Under E13 it is left unsaid, and the sets
  say "every positive mass" there. So the two output types differ by who speaks for the share $1-M$:
  all of it at the all-zero experiment, none at strong signal.
- **The starting law as an input [TASK §5; O6].** $1-M$ is a number for how much of a result is the
  declared starting law's: the branch-cut test's "its influence vanishes where the readings identify" in
  one figure, for this construction.
- **The discount [O14].** The far pair has $M=1$: the ranks are as sure about it as about the near pair.
  Like the Gaussian curve, they carry no discount (T75 (2)).
