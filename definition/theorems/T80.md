## Standard mathematics

Gleser and Hwang (1987): in an errors-in-variables model, and for a ratio of means, a confidence set
that is bounded with probability one has confidence level zero. Dufour (1997): the same for any
parameter that is not identified somewhere on the closure of the model. Fieller (1954): the exact set
for a ratio is an interval, the complement of an interval, or everything, according to whether the
denominator can be told from zero. Mass from force and acceleration is a ratio, restricted to positive
values.

## Statement

**(1) An always-finite set is wrong about large masses when the signal is weak, however wide it is.**
Fix any finite series of declarations. When every true push is zero the readings have one law, the
same for every mass. So for any procedure whose set of masses is bounded with probability one, the
probability that the set holds the true mass tends to zero as the true mass grows. With weak pushes in
place of zero ones the same holds, because their readings' law is as close to the no-signal law as one
likes. The central interval of any proper law of mass is such a set. No rate of widening helps: what
fails is being finite every time. A set that is right with probability at least $p$ for every mass and
every signal must be unbounded on readings of positive probability.

**(2) What that forces.** From three things:

- every declared σ is positive, so an acceleration can never be ruled out as zero (L2);
- nothing promises a signal of any size, so arbitrarily weak signals are in scope (L10);
- the quoted uncertainty is a true statement for every true mass (property N, read mass by mass),

the quoted uncertainty is a set of masses that can be unbounded. The first two are on the premise
list. The third has one alternative: "true on average over a starting law". Then a proper law with a
finite interval is admissible, because it only promises to be right about masses the starting law
finds plausible. So the statement is a dichotomy: **either the starting law is a fact about the object,
or the output is a set that can be unbounded.**

*Sort (provisional).* The second. Three reasons from the pages. Property N's own words are "we become
sure that we don't know anything about the actual mass of the thing", and a finite interval, however
wide, says something about it. No argument forces a starting law's shape after three routes closed
(`../../problems/O6.md`), and past one instrument its centre is declared and not derived
(`../../problems/O11.md`). And an input must be something practice supplies (TASK §5): nobody declares
one.

**(3) The set has four shapes, decided by which channel can be told from zero.** For one scalar reading
the curve (T73) is $F^2/\sigma_F^2$ at mass 0 and $a^2/\sigma_a^2$ at infinite mass, and 0 at the reading's own
ratio. Cut at a threshold $q$:

| acceleration beyond $\sqrt q$ σ of zero | force beyond $\sqrt q$ σ of zero | the kept masses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yes | yes | an interval |
| no | yes | "at least so much": $F=10\pm1$, $a=1\pm1$ gives 3.28 upward |
| yes | no | "at most so much" |
| no | no | every positive mass |

For a series the same holds with $K(\infty)-K_{\min}$ and $K(0)-K_{\min}$ in place of the two squared
readings. The exact tests of T75 have the same four shapes at their own thresholds.

**(4) The passage between them is continuous.** In the noise angle, where infinite mass is the end of
the quarter turn, the ends of the kept set are continuous in the readings. The upper end reaches the
end of the arc at finite data: when the acceleration comes within $\sqrt q$ σ of zero (1.96 σ at 95%).
"No interval" is not a separate mode; it is the arc having grown to the whole quarter turn.

**(5) Where every mass is kept, nothing is owed as a point.** Any value returned there would be a
convention (R-2026-11-22-2: "if it is the case that every value is compatible, it really doesn't
matter what you return right?"). The all-zero experiment is the identity of combination (T73 (2)),
which is all the 29 Sept argument needs (`../../problems/O20.md`, finding 1).

## Proof

(1) With all pushes zero the law $P_0$ of the readings does not depend on the mass. For a procedure with
set $S(Y)$, $P_0\{m\in S(Y)\}\le P_0\{\sup S(Y)\ge m\}\to0$ as $m\to\infty$ when $\sup S<\infty$ almost surely.
For nonzero pushes shrinking to zero at a fixed mass the readings' laws tend to $P_0$ in total
variation, which bounds the change in the probability of any event (T45 §4; re-derived here). (3) the
curve of one scalar reading is $(F-ma)^2/(\sigma_F^2+m^2\sigma_a^2)$, monotone on each side of its zero on
the positive masses. (4) in the noise angle the curve is $|z|^2\sin^2(\theta-\theta_z)$, and the roots of
a sinusoid at a level move continuously with its amplitude and phase.

## Measured (`tests/test_unbounded_sets.py`)

Five noise-only scalar readings, 300 series. The 95% interval of a proper law (T78's witness, centre 1)
holds the mass 1 in every series and the mass $10^4$ in none. The exact conditional test (T75) keeps the
mass $10^4$ in 0.963 of them.

## What it does and does not settle

- **Settles, provisionally, G28** (what the quoted width promises on noise): it promises to hold the true
  mass with at least its stated probability for every mass and every signal, and so it is a set that
  can be unbounded. "We know nothing" shows as every positive mass being kept.
- **Puts the starting law's question in one place.** The law-and-point output is exactly the other horn:
  it is a true statement relative to a starting law. `../type.md` keeps it as the old line's output.
  A caller who does hold a starting law can multiply it onto the curve; that is then their input.
- **Does not choose the set.** Statistic, null, weight, level (T75, T76; `../../problems/O20.md`,
  finding 5).
- **Does not touch the discount** (`../../problems/O14.md`), or what noise-only readings do (T77).
- **Does not say the output is only a set.** The curve and the leftover are beside it (T73).
